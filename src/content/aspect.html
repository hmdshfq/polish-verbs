Chapter 02 · the heart of the system

Aspect
finished, or not

Every Polish verb comes in a pair, and the two halves do not differ in tense. They differ in completion: one describes an action as a process, a habit or something still going on; the other presents it as a single act that is, or will be, done. Learn this one distinction properly and most of what looks hard about Polish verbs stops being hard.

01

The pair

Take pisać. It is not one verb with several uses; it is one half of a pair, and the other half is napisać. They share a meaning — to write — but they answer a question the English word leaves open: is the writing a process we are looking at, or a piece of writing that now exists?

{{conj:pisać}} {{conj:napisać}}
AspectPolish nameWhat it presentsThe question it answers
imperfective impfniedokonany An action as a process, a habit, a repeated act, or something unfinished. “What were you doing?” — Co robiłeś?
perfective pfdokonany A single act viewed as a whole, with a result: started, finished, or done once. “What did you do?” — Co zrobiłeś?
The test that works

Ask: can I put “for two hours” on it, or does it need “in two hours”? A process lasts (pisałem list przez dwie godziny), a completed act is reached (napisałem list w dwie godziny). English marks this with verb forms; Polish marks it with a different word.

02

What aspect changes in a sentence

The same event can be reported with either half, and the two reports are both true. What changes is what the speaker puts in front of you.

SentenceWhat it puts in front of you
Wczoraj pisałem list.I was writing a letter — the writing, possibly unfinished.
Wczoraj napisałem list.I wrote a letter — and it is written.
Czytałem tę książkę.I was reading this book / I have read in it. You may not have finished it.
Przeczytałem tę książkę.I read this book through, to the end.
Kupowałem chleb.I was buying bread / I used to buy bread.
Kupiłem chleb.I bought the bread — one purchase, done.

Notice that aspect is not the same as tense. Pisałem and napisałem are both past. Piszę and napiszę look alike but are present and future — because a perfective verb has no present (chapter 3). The two systems run side by side and answer different questions.

03

How the pairs are made

There are four ways a pair can be built. Recognising which one you are looking at is what lets you predict a partner you have not met before.

1. Prefix: pisać → napisać

The perfective adds a prefix to the imperfective. This is the commonest pattern, and the prefix usually keeps a trace of its own meaning — na- = onto, done to completion; z- = done; prze- = through.

{{pairs:prefix}}
The trap inside the pattern

Prefixes also make new words. Pisać → napisać is a pair, but pisać → podpisać is not: podpisać means “to sign”, a different action that happens to use the same root. When you meet a prefixed verb, ask whether it means the same thing as the base. If it does, it is probably the partner. If it does not, you have found a new verb.

2. Suffix: kupić → kupować

Here the direction is the other way round: the imperfective is the longer word. The suffixes are -ować, -ywać, -ać and -awać. This is the biggest family among verbs that describe single acts, and the rule to hold on to is short: the longer verb is the one that repeats or continues.

{{pairs:suffix}}

3. Suppletive: brać → wziąć

Two unrelated roots. There is no rule to apply and nothing to predict — the pair has to be learnt whole. There are only a handful, but they are among the most used verbs in the language.

{{pairs:suppletive}}

4. No partner at all

Some imperfective verbs have no perfective, because their meaning does not allow an end point: być (to be), mieć (to have), wiedzieć (to know a fact), znać (to know a person or place), mieszkać (to live somewhere). These are states, not acts. Where English says “to get”, Polish uses a different verb: dostać, not a perfective of mieć.

Where a pair is lopsided

A few entries are marked with a note rather than a clean partner. Chodzić and jeździć are the repeated, back-and-forth partners of iść and jechać, but their own perfective is the same word: pójść / pojechać. Stać (to be standing) pairs with stanąć (to take a stand), which is not simply “to finish standing”. Pracować has no plain completing partner at all.

05

Aspect and tense together

Because a perfective verb has no present, the two aspects fill the three tenses in different ways. This little table is worth memorising on its own; it explains why napiszę is future and będę pisać is also future, but they are not the same promise.

Imperfective — pisaćPerfective — napisać
pastpisałem / pisałamnapisałem / napisałam
presentpiszę— none —
futurebędę pisać / będę pisał(a)napiszę

So “I will write” has two translations, and they are not interchangeable. Napiszę promises a finished letter. Będę pisać tells you what I will be busy with. Chapter 5 takes this apart in detail.

06

Six traps, in the order you will hit them

1
Learning one half. Look up “buy”, write down kupić, and then be lost in the past, where the habitual buying is kupowałem. Write the pair.
2
Thinking the past tense is imperfective. “I bought” is kupiłem, a perfective past. The past can be either aspect; it is the aspect that says whether the purchase was completed.
3
Putting być in front of a perfective. Będę napisać is not Polish. The compound future takes an imperfective: będę pisać. A perfective makes its own future.
4
Expecting a prefix to mean completion only. Napisać completes writing; podpisać means to sign, przepisać means to copy out. New word, not new aspect.
5
Using a perfective after zawsze, często, co dzień. A habit repeats, and repetition belongs to the imperfective: Zawsze piszę rano, never zawsze napiszę.
6
Using a perfective in a negative command. Nie rób tego!, not nie zrób tego. Prohibitions take the imperfective (chapter 6).
