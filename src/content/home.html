How this site is built

The cases site taught you to ask what job a word is doing. A verb rewards the same question, answered in three steps. Every chapter on this site follows them, so you always know where you are.

AskWhat it decidesExample: napisać
1. Is it finished? Aspect — the pair of the verb, imperfective or perfective. pisać → napisać: a process, then the finished result
2. Who is doing it? The person ending, and the past tense's gender. napisałem / napisałam — a man wrote, a woman wrote
3. What does it take? Its case, its preposition, its reflexive się. napisać list (accusative) — to write a letter
The one thing to learn first

Polish verbs come in pairs, and the difference between the two halves is not tense but completion. Get that idea and the rest of the system stops being a pile of endings: it becomes two clear sets of forms. Chapter 2 is nothing else.

Where the grammar comes from

Polish grammar is well described, and it is worth saying exactly which description this site follows, so you can check it.

1
Every form is checked, not remembered. Each conjugation table on this site is generated from the inflection table of that verb on English Wiktionary, which is built from the SGJP (Grammatical Dictionary of Polish) — the machine-readable description maintained by the Polish Academy of Sciences' Institute of Computer Science. If the source does not give a form, the site does not print one.
2
The rules are stated in the words Polish teachers use. The names of the cases, the two aspects (aspekt dokonany and niedokonany), the three tenses, tryb rozkazujący and przypuszczający, and rekcja (the case a verb takes) are the terms you will meet in a classroom in Łódź, not invented ones.
3
The doubt is written down. Where good Polish allows two forms, or where a rule has a real exception, the site says so in a note rather than picking one and hiding the other. A learner who meets the second form later should not think the site was wrong.
