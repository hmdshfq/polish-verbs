Chapter 12 · from verb to adjective, and where the verb sits

Participles and word order
the forms that stop being verbs

A participle is a verb that has taken on an adjective's endings, or an adverb's job. Polish has four of them, and they let you say “the man writing the letter” and “the letter written yesterday” without a relative clause. This last chapter also covers the question every learner asks by the end: with all these endings, does word order matter at all?

One rule that saves you half the work

The active adjectival participle and the contemporary adverbial participle exist only for imperfective verbs. There is no “having written” adjective from napisać — you cannot say napiszący. This is not an exception; it follows from what a perfective verb means. A completed act has no ongoing “while doing it” version.

02

The active adjectival participle: -ący

Built on the third person plural of the present, with -cy where the verb has -ą. It declines exactly like an adjective, so it agrees with the noun it describes.

It agrees, so it changes

Człowiek piszący list (masculine), kobieta pisząca list (feminine), dzieci piszące list (neuter / mixed plural). These are adjective endings, nothing more: -y, -a, -e.

03

The passive adjectival participle: -ny, -ony, -ty

This is the one you will see most, because it is also how Polish forms the passive voice. It describes something that has been done to it, and it is built from transitive verbs — both aspects.

Some participles are irregular

Otworzyć → otwarty, not “otworzony”. Zamknąć → zamknięty. Wziąć → wzięty. These are old forms and have to be learnt with the verb; a table is safer than a guess, which is exactly what the verb finder is for.

The passive voice, in one line

być (or zostać) + passive participle, with the participle agreeing with the subject: List jest napisany, Książka jest napisana, Okno jest napisane, Listy są napisane. Zostać emphasises the change: List został napisany — the letter came to be written.

04

The adverbial participle: -ąc

This one never changes — no gender, no number, no case. It describes an action happening at the same time as the main verb, and it belongs to the same subject.

VerbParticipleSentenceMeaning
czytaćczytającCzytając gazetę, piłem kawę.Reading the paper, I was drinking coffee.
iśćidącIdąc do pracy, myślałem o tobie.Walking to work, I was thinking about you.
robićrobiącRobił to, nie wiedząc, po co.He did it without knowing why.
mówićmówiącMówiąc szczerze, nie wiem.Frankly speaking, I don't know.
byćbędącBędąc w Łodzi, odwiedź muzeum.While in Łódź, visit the museum.
The subject must be the same

Czytając gazetę, piłem kawę is fine: I was both reading and drinking. But you cannot use it when the two actions have different subjects — the participle always refers to the subject of the main verb. Polish is strict about this in careful writing.

The “having done” form

There is also an anterior adverbial participle for a completed action: zrobiwszy, przeczytawszy, napisawszy. It is literary and old-fashioned. Recognise it in books; do not use it in speech. Use a normal clause instead: Kiedy przeczytałem książkę, poszedłem spać.

05

The verbal noun: -nie and -cie

Every verb has a noun built from its infinitive stem, with -nie (or -cie after a consonant that does not take n). It is a neuter noun, it declines like one, and it takes the genitive of its object.

VerbNounIn a sentenceMeaning
pisaćpisaniePisanie listów zajmuje czas.Writing letters takes time.
czytaćczytanieCzytanie książek jest przyjemne.Reading books is pleasant.
kupowaćkupowanieKupowanie prezentów to przyjemność.Buying presents is a pleasure.
jeśćjedzenieJedzenie jest ważne.Eating is important.
myćmycieMycie rąk jest konieczne.Washing hands is necessary.
pićpiciePicie wody jest zdrowe.Drinking water is healthy.
Watch two things

First: the object of the noun goes genitive — pisanie listów, not pisanie listy, because a noun governs the genitive where a verb governed the accusative. Second: some of these nouns have taken on a life of their own: jedzenie is “food”, mieszkanie is “flat”, and picie can be “drink”.

06

Word order: flexible, not free

Polish endings carry the grammar, so position carries emphasis. That is why the same sentence has several correct orders — and why each one says something slightly different. The neutral order is subject, verb, object.

OrderSentenceWhat it answers
S–V–O (neutral)Anna czyta książkę.“What's Anna doing?” — Anna is reading a book.
O–V–SKsiążkę czyta Anna.“Who's reading the book?” — Anna is.
V–O–SCzyta książkę Anna.same emphasis as above, slightly more marked
O–S–VKsiążkę Anna czyta.“the book — Anna is reading it” (as opposed to something else)
V–S–OCzyta Anna książkę.neutral in the negative-elided reading; also “Anna is reading”
The working rule

The last element carries the new information. If you want to stress who did something, put them late; if you want to stress what was done, put the thing late. When you are not stressing anything, use subject–verb–object and you will never be wrong.

07

The small words have fixed places

Word order is flexible for the big pieces, but the little ones sit where they sit. Getting these right is what makes a sentence sound Polish rather than translated.

1
nie goes directly before the verb. Nie czytam, nie jestem zmęczony. Never Czytam nie.
2
się follows the verb. Myję się, uczymy się. Never first in a sentence.
3
Short object pronouns do not start a sentence. Widzę cię, daj mi to. If the pronoun must come first, use the long form: Ciebie widzę.
4
by attaches to the past form, or to the front word. Zrobiłbym to or Ja bym to zrobił — but not Ja zrobiłbym bym.
5
że never comes first in its clause. Myślę, że masz rację — the że part is a separate clause with its own order.

09

Four traps

1
A participle from a perfective verb. Napiszący does not exist. The active and -ąc participles come from imperfectives only.
2
A participle that does not agree. List jest napisan**a** is wrong: the participle agrees with the noun it describes, so napisany for a letter, napisana for a book.
3
A verbal noun with an accusative object. A noun governs the genitive: czytanie książek, not czytanie książki.
4
Rearranging words for no reason. A scrambled word order is grammatical but says something you may not mean. When in doubt, use subject–verb–object.
10

What to take away

  • -ący and -ąc come from imperfective verbs only.
  • The passive participle (-ny / -ony / -ty) agrees like an adjective and makes the passive with być or zostać.
  • The adverbial participle always describes the same subject as the main verb.
  • The verbal noun takes the genitive: czytanie książek.
  • Word order is flexible because endings carry the grammar — the last element carries the emphasis.
